How Gatecast works

Gatecast is weird news from real outlets, with the weather on top, a library of strange true stories and the classics they sit beside, and flight and trip checks when you need them. This page says how the stories and the weather verdicts are chosen, how reader votes and tips work, where every piece comes from, what we never do, and how to tell us when we get something wrong.

How the weird news is chosen

Every story comes from a real news outlet. We show its own headline, its name, the time and a link to the full story on its site. We don’t copy the article, rewrite the headline or show the outlet’s page inside ours. Pictures are the outlet’s own preview images, loaded from its site and credited, and a few outlets are shown without pictures. A story with no picture and no video is shown as text, with no empty frame.

When you click a story, it opens here on “Gatecast TV”, a full-screen story view, instead of sending you away. It shows the outlet’s headline, its preview picture (or a video, which loads only after you press play), the editor’s hook and, for the editor’s picks, a short summary in our own words, marked “In our words”. The full story is always at the outlet: the big “Read the full story at …” button links straight to it. Each story has its own link (it ends in ?story= and a short code) that you can share; your browser’s Back button, Escape or the Close button take you back to where you were on the page. With reduced motion turned on in your device settings, the channel static and the title card are replaced by a simple fade.

Some stories have a video: one the outlet put in its article, or one our editor added to a pick. We show videos from YouTube, Vimeo, Facebook, X, TikTok and Instagram, always in the provider’s own player and never copied to our servers, and only videos the provider lets other sites show. Until you press play, nothing on the page contacts any of them: for YouTube, Vimeo and TikTok the still image is fetched by our server and served to you by Gatecast; for Facebook, X and Instagram you see our own poster instead. Before we show a YouTube, Vimeo, X or TikTok video we ask that service that it still exists and may be shown on other sites; Facebook only answers that question to registered apps, so its videos come with a link to the post in case the player will not start. Instagram videos cannot be shown on other sites, so we link to the post instead.

When you press play, the provider’s player loads from its own address, and from then on the provider sees your request as it would if you watched the video on its site: YouTube’s privacy-enhanced player (youtube-nocookie.com), Vimeo’s player with its “do not track” setting on (player.vimeo.com), Facebook’s video player (facebook.com), X’s post embed and its script (platform.twitter.com), or TikTok’s player (tiktok.com). Each one can see your IP address and set its own cookies under its own rules. Every video carries a credit, such as “Video: channel on Vimeo”, and a link to the original post.

The outlets are UPI Odd News, Smithsonian Magazine, Atlas Obscura, ScienceDaily, Mental Floss, Live Science, The Conversation, SoraNews24, Sky News and Oddity Central. General-interest outlets only appear when a story is genuinely odd, and their quizzes, lists and podcasts are left out.

Our rule is simple: the strangeness has to be in the situation, not in someone’s misfortune. Automatic checks leave out stories about deaths, injuries and illness, animals being harmed, mental illness, children, crime and court cases, disasters, sexual content, and ordinary people who went viral. The checks can only remove stories, never add them, and when in doubt they leave a story out.

The editor’s picks come first. A person chose each one, and any rating on it is the editor’s own. After the picks, the outlets take turns, fresher stories before older ones and each outlet’s oddest first, and no single outlet fills more than 3 of the next 12 stories or 10 of the next 30 while others have stories to show. Clicks never change the order of anything on the page.

Some picks have a hook: one short line under the headline, at most 140 characters. An AI assistant (Claude, made by Anthropic) may draft it from the outlet’s headline, or from the story itself when the editor asks it to read the page. A person checks it, edits it as needed and approves every hook before it appears. A hook should make you wonder, but it must not say more than the story does, mock anyone or make light of harm.

Some picks also have a summary: two to four sentences in our own words, shown as “In our words” when you open the story. The same assistant may draft it from the headline, facts the editor types in and, when the editor asks, the story page; a person edits and approves every summary. It may only use facts the outlet reported, and it is checked so that it never copies the outlet’s writing: no run of eight words or more from the headline or the article. Some sites turn automated readers away (UPI’s does); we don’t try to get around that, and the editor writes from what they know.

The favourites row under the lead follows the editor’s order. A story with 10 or more Weird-o-meter votes from the past 7 days is ranked by readers’ votes instead and goes first, and while one is, the row is headed “Reader favourites — ranked by Weird-o-meter votes” instead of “Editor’s favourites”.

Reader votes and tips

The Weird-o-meter and the guestbook don’t use accounts or cookies. To count one vote per person per story and to stop floods, we keep a scrambled code (a salted SHA-256 hash) made from your network address, never the address itself. A vote’s code is deleted 90 days after you last voted on that story, while your rating stays in the story’s total. A tip’s code changes every day and is erased after 7 days, and the tip itself is deleted after 90 days.

The readers’ average appears only once 50 people have voted on a story. We never open the links you send; a person checks every one before anything is published. Your reading streak is kept on your device only.

We also count clicks on the weird stories, so the editor can see which ones land. When you follow a link to a weird story’s original (“Read it at …” on the front page, or “Read the full story at …” in a story you opened), your browser sends us the story’s address, and we add one to that story’s total for the day. No cookie is set, and the total holds nothing about you: no address, no code made from it, no time of day. To stop one machine inflating the counts, a scrambled code of your network address, which changes every day, is held in a rate counter (at most 120 clicks an hour) for up to two days, without the story. If your browser sends Do Not Track or Global Privacy Control, nothing is sent at all. Daily totals are kept for 90 days. Only the editor sees them, and they never rank anything: not the weird stories, not political items, not The brief.

Counting visits

We count visits to each page in total with Vercel Web Analytics, from Vercel, the company that hosts Gatecast. It uses no cookies and stores nothing in your browser. It tells us how many people opened a page, which site sent them, and their country, browser and type of device, never who they are. To tell one visit from the next it uses a scrambled code made from your request, which is thrown away after 24 hours, so it cannot follow you from one day to the next or from one site to another. The counter’s script comes from Gatecast’s own address, not from another site. Vercel explains what it collects in its Web Analytics privacy notes.

If your browser sends Do Not Track or Global Privacy Control, the counter doesn’t load at all, the same rule as for the story clicks above. Apart from these two counts, Gatecast measures nothing: no advertising or social media trackers, no session recordings, no heatmaps. (A video’s provider can see you once you press play, as described under videos.)

The editor’s picks are also published week by week and as an RSS feed; neither tells us who reads them. If you sign up for the weekly email, Buttondown keeps your address and sends the email for us, with its open and click tracking turned off, and every email has an unsubscribe link.

How The brief is chosen

The brief on the front page is built from four things: the place you chose, the weather there (National Weather Service alerts and the forecast at the airports near you), the US state that place is in, and a trip saved on this device, which only adds a note when an alert covers your departure. It shows only the parts that have something in them. There is no hidden profile: the place and trips are stored in your browser, not on our servers.

Weather news

Active National Weather Service alerts for your place, most severe first, each linked to weather.gov. Then at most one forecast item, and only when an airport near you is at Elevated or High risk in the next three hours. A calm day adds nothing.

Headlines

From a hand-picked list of 20 newsrooms (listed below). For a US place in a state on the list you get that state’s newsrooms; otherwise a short list of weather and aviation newsrooms. Stories from the past seven days, newest first, at most three from any one outlet and eight in all.

Political items are never ranked by clicks. Clicks on these headlines are not counted (the only clicks Gatecast counts are anonymous totals on the weird stories, for the editor), and the order is time.

Nothing copied

Each headline is the outlet’s own words, its name, the time and a link to the story. We never read or show the description, the opening paragraph or the article. There are no AI-written summaries or rewritten headlines.

Pictures

The picture beside a headline is the publisher’s own link-preview image, credited “Image: outlet”. Your browser loads it straight from the publisher’s site (we never copy, store or resize it), and no referrer is sent. That means the publisher’s server sees your IP address. Any outlet can be switched to text only, and a headline whose picture fails to load is simply shown without one.

Your statehouse

Recent floor votes on bills in your state, copied as recorded from Open States: the bill, its official title, the result and the split by party, linked to the official record. Committee votes, procedural motions and votes older than 120 days are left out, and we add no words of our own.

Both sides

One full, dated, linked quote from a named Democrat and one from a named Republican on the same topic, under a neutral fact with its source. We do not paraphrase or grade either. A person approves every topic before it appears, and the database refuses to publish a topic without a statement from each side. Every publish, withdrawal and deletion is logged with who did it.

How a weather verdict is worked out

Gatecast looks at the hourly forecast for the hours that matter: for a trip, the hours you are at each airport, from an hour before you land to an hour after you leave; for a flight, the time around departure and landing; for an airport, the next three hours. Each hour gets points for weather that tends to slow airports down: thunderstorms, freezing rain, snow, fog and low visibility, strong gusts and very heavy rain. The worst factor counts in full and each further one adds a third of its points. The worst hour sets the level.

  • Low under 30 points
  • Elevated 30 to 59
  • High 60 and above

At a connection, weather where your inbound flight leaves counts at half weight, because a late inbound flight is what breaks a connection, and a short layover adds points. A short layover on its own is pointed out in words, not counted as weather risk.

These thresholds are judgement calls. They have not yet been back-tested against real delay records, and they will change when they are.

A trip verdict also says how far ahead it is: up to three days the forecast is usually good, up to a week timing can still shift, and beyond that it is an outlook. A sharper confidence label, from how many forecast model runs agree, is planned but not built yet.

How the books are chosen

The Stacks, our library, holds real, published books chosen one by one by our editor. Six shelves are true stories: True Crime, Serial Killers · Case Files, Unsolved Mysteries, Weird History, Strange Science, and Cults & Cons. Two, Fantasy & Magic and Science Fiction Classics, are fiction: the classic novels the true stories sit beside, never presented as fact. Every shelf and book is also on a plain page: The Stacks, as a list.

A book goes on a shelf when it tells its story well and fits that shelf. Being disputed does not rule a book out: some of the best-known true stories are also the most argued over, so we keep them and say what is disputed. Nobody pays for a place on a shelf.

True crime
Our true-crime shelves are chosen for the investigation and the victims, never to make killers famous: the books focus on the cases and the people who solved them. Every book on True Crime and Serial Killers · Case Files carries a content note, and a book whose usual cover shows a killer or a cult leader gets our own stand-in cover instead.
Titles, authors and years
Our editor checks the title, authors and year of every book. The year is when it first came out in book form; where a book was serialised first, has another title in another country or was later revised, we say so on its page. We compare our records with Open Library’s, and a cover is shown only when its title and author match ours.
Descriptions, content notes and “Worth knowing”
Every description is ours, written for Gatecast, never a publisher’s blurb. A content note says what a book describes, such as real killings, abuse or self-harm, so you can decide before you start. “Worth knowing” records a known, published dispute about a book’s accuracy or fairness, or something about its editions worth having before you look for a copy. Every note is also in one place: content notes and Worth knowing.
Shelf introductions and Gatecast’s take
Each shelf page opens with a short introduction, and some books have Gatecast’s take: why the book is on the shelf, what it does well, what to keep in mind and where to start. These are drafted with an AI assistant (Claude, made by Anthropic), then checked, edited as needed and approved by our editor before they appear. They keep to facts on the public record and never quote the books.
Books put forward by authors
Authors and publishers can suggest a book for the true-story shelves; the fiction shelves are the editor’s choice alone. The editor decides, and writes the description and notes; nothing from the pitch is published. A book that joins a shelf this way carries a small “Suggested by the author” or “Suggested by the publisher” note. See books put forward by authors for what we keep and for how long.
Book of the Week
One book from the shelves, chosen by our editor with a one-line reason; nobody pays for the spot. See Book of the Week.
The links on each book
“Find at your library” is a WorldCat search for the title and author. “Buy at Bookshop.org” is a plain search on Bookshop.org, not an affiliate link, so we earn nothing from it. “Open Library record” goes to the book’s record there once the match is confirmed, and to an Open Library search until then. Covers load from Open Library; nothing else from these sites loads until you follow a link. Previews work differently: see book previews.

Where the data comes from

Every source is free and public. Each has limits, and we would rather show nothing than guess.

Open-Meteo
Forecasts up to 16 days, place search and air quality, called from your browser. Licensed CC BY 4.0. Its free tier is for non-commercial use and is limited per network address, so the forecast can be busy at times.
National Weather Service
Weather alerts, and which state a US place is in. US only.
NOAA Aviation Weather Center
The latest observation at an airport (METAR), through our server. The raw report is one tap deeper.
FAA NAS Status
Delay programs in force at US airports now, and what the FAA plans for today: ground stops and delay programs it expects (from its daily operations plan) and de-icing. The FAA publishes these times in UTC; we show them in the airport’s local time. A planned item is what the FAA expects, not a certainty. These feeds are not documented and can change, so if one stops working the line simply disappears. Raw airport notices such as night closures are left out. US only.
adsb.lol and adsbdb
A flight’s live position from community aircraft receivers (adsb.lol, ODbL) and its route (adsbdb). Aircraft out of receiver range do not show. Neither gives scheduled times, gates, delays or cancellations, so we do not show those; the landing time is our estimate from distance and speed.
RainViewer
Past radar on the front page, free for personal and small-scale use, over OpenStreetMap map tiles.
Open States
Statehouse floor votes, collected once an hour, a few states at a time. Votes can arrive late, and we link the official record on every one.
Newsrooms
The headlines and preview pictures come from these outlets’ public feeds, checked on 16 November 2026:
  • New York: New York Focus, City & State New York, WAMC
  • New Jersey: New Jersey Monitor, NJ Spotlight News
  • California: CalMatters, KQED
  • Ohio: Ohio Capital Journal, Statehouse News Bureau
  • Texas: The Texas Tribune, KUT News
  • Florida: Florida Phoenix, WFSU News
  • Illinois: Capitol News Illinois, NPR Illinois
  • Pennsylvania: Pennsylvania Capital-Star, Spotlight PA
  • Weather and travel, everywhere else: Yale Climate Connections, NOAA, Simple Flying
Other states do not have local headlines yet.
YouTube, for videos in the weird news
Our server asks YouTube’s public oEmbed service whether a video exists and may be embedded, and keeps its title and channel name for the label. The still image is fetched by our server from YouTube’s image server and served to you by Gatecast (cached for a day); the front page itself contacts no YouTube address until you press play. Then the player loads from youtube-nocookie.com. See videos above.
Book previews in The Stacks
Some books have a Preview: an official video about the book (an author interview, a talk, or a film trailer, labelled as such) and a look inside from the publisher through Google Books or from the Internet Archive. Each one is checked and approved by our editor first. Opening a book contacts none of these services. When you open Preview, the video’s still image comes from YouTube; the video loads from YouTube’s privacy-enhanced player (youtube-nocookie.com) only when you press play, and the pages load from Google Books or the Internet Archive only when you open them. From that moment that service sees your request, as it would if you visited it. C-SPAN, PBS and Criterion videos are links to their own sites (c-span.org, pbs.org, criterion.com) and are not played on Gatecast.
Books put forward by authors
Authors and publishers can suggest a book for The Stacks with the form at the end of the library page. We take real, published true stories with an ISBN that fit one of the true-story shelves; there is no payment and no promised place. Our editor reads every one, and if a book goes on a shelf, the editor writes its description, notes and topics: nothing from the pitch is published. Those books carry a small “Suggested by the author” or “Suggested by the publisher” note. We keep what you send until the editor decides. Your email address, if you leave one, is deleted 90 days after the decision, a book we turn down is deleted after 180 days, and the scrambled code of your network address that stops floods is erased after 7 days.
Book of the Week
Our editor picks one book from the shelves each week and says why in one line; it stands on top of the bookcases until the next pick, and nobody pays for the spot.
Your location
Asked for only when you press “Use my location”. It is rounded to about a kilometre, kept in your browser and sent only to the weather services that need it (Open-Meteo, and the National Weather Service in the US). The place name comes from the nearest airport in a list bundled with the site.

What Gatecast never does

  • Show news text written by AI. Headlines are the outlets’ own and quotes are word for word. An AI assistant may help a reviewer check quotes against their sources, and may draft the short hook line and the “In our words” summary for a weird-news pick, and The Stacks’ shelf introductions and Gatecast’s takes on books; nothing it writes is saved unless a person checks it, edits it as needed and approves it, and it never writes a headline or a quote.
  • Sell your data. There are no accounts; your place and trips stay in your browser.
  • Rank political items by clicks, or grade newsrooms as left, right or trustworthy.
  • Show gates, scheduled times, delays or cancellations it cannot know. Delay information comes only from the FAA and is labelled as the FAA’s; the landing time is always marked as an estimate.
  • Pretend to be official. Gatecast is a planning aid: check your airline for the status of your flight.

Corrections and contact

If something on Gatecast is wrong, misleading or out of date, please tell us. For now reports go through GitHub, where they are public: Report an error. Say which page, what you saw and what it should say. We fix errors as soon as we can; a public list of corrections is planned.

Security problems should not go in a public report. Use GitHub’s private vulnerability reporting instead. There is no email address yet.

Newsrooms: if you would like your pictures or headlines removed, report it the same way and we will switch your outlet off.
